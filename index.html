Evidence for the nucleus

Rutherford scattering

Alpha particles fired at a single nucleus, with paths calculated from Coulomb repulsion. Change the alpha energy and the target to see how close they get and how sharply they turn.

view 500 fm wide · nucleus drawn to scale

Key equation

closest approach (head-on): Ek = QαQN / 4πε₀d

Breaking it down

  1. Set-up: a beam of alpha particles in a vacuum hits thin gold foil; a detector (zinc sulfide screen and microscope) is moved around to count particles at each angle.
  2. Most go straight through → most of the atom is empty space.
  3. A few are deflected by large angles → there is a concentrated, positive charge that repels the positive alphas.
  4. A very few (about 1 in 10 000) bounce back more than 90° → nearly all the mass and positive charge is in a tiny nucleus.
  5. At the closest approach in a head-on collision, all the alpha's kinetic energy has become electric potential energy. This gives an upper limit for the nuclear radius.

Common traps

  • Saying the alphas "hit" the nucleus. They are repelled before touching it.
  • Charge of an alpha is +2e; charge of the nucleus is +Ze. Don't forget either factor.
  • MeV to J: multiply by 1.60 × 10⁻¹³.
  • The foil is thin so each alpha is scattered by at most one nucleus.

Try this: double the energy. What happens to the closest approach distance? Then switch to aluminium.

Randomness and half-life

Radioactive decay

400 unstable nuclei, each decaying at random. The graph compares the actual number left with the exponential prediction. Run it a few times: the curve is never quite the same.

■ undecayed · ■ decayed

Key equations

A = λNN = N₀e−λtA = A₀e−λtT½ = ln 2 / λ

Breaking it down

  1. Decay is random (you can't predict which nucleus decays next) and spontaneous (not affected by temperature, pressure or chemistry).
  2. Every nucleus has the same probability of decaying per second: the decay constant λ. So the rate of decay is proportional to N: A = λN.
  3. That leads to exponential decay. Half-life is the time for N (or A) to halve; it is the same however many nuclei you start with.
  4. Take logs: ln N = ln N₀ − λt. A graph of ln N against t is a straight line with gradient −λ.
  5. N–Z plot: nuclei with too many neutrons undergo β⁻ decay; too many protons undergo β⁺ decay or electron capture; very heavy nuclei emit alpha particles.
  6. Medical tracers (e.g. technetium-99m) need a short half-life (hours), emit γ only, and should be easy to produce on site.

Common traps

  • λ must be in s⁻¹ when A is in Bq. Convert years to seconds (1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s).
  • N is the number of nuclei, not the mass: N = (mass / molar mass) × NA.
  • Half-life is the time to halve, not the time for half to decay "on average".
  • Count rate isn't activity: detectors only catch some of the radiation, and you must subtract background.

Try this: switch to ln N and check the gradient against −λ in the readout. Why does the line get ragged at the end?

Required Practical 12

α, β, γ and the inverse-square law

A source, an optional absorber and a GM tube. Choose the radiation, move the detector, and record readings to test the inverse-square law for gamma. Counts are random, as in the real experiment.

background ≈ 0.40 counts s⁻¹

Key equation

γ from a point source: I = k / x²corrected count = measured − background

Breaking it down

  1. α: helium nucleus, very ionising, range a few cm in air, stopped by paper.
  2. β⁻: fast electron, moderately ionising, range up to about 1 m in air, stopped by a few mm of aluminium.
  3. γ: EM radiation, weakly ionising, reduced (not stopped) by several cm of lead. In air it obeys the inverse-square law because it spreads out over the surface of a sphere.
  4. To identify a source: measure background, then the count rate with paper, then aluminium, then lead in the way, and see which absorber cuts it down.
  5. To test inverse square: correct for background, then plot corrected count rate against 1/x². A straight line through the origin supports I ∝ 1/x².
  6. The real distance to the source may be offset by an unknown amount d: plot 1/√C against measured x; the x-intercept gives −d.

Common traps

  • Forgetting to subtract background before using inverse square.
  • Saying lead "stops" gamma. It reduces the intensity.
  • Inverse square only applies to γ. α and β are absorbed by air.
  • Safety: handle sources with tongs, keep them in a lead-lined box when not in use, keep at arm's length and minimise time of exposure.

Try this: with γ and no absorber, record readings at 5, 10, 15, 20, 30 and 40 cm. Do the points lie on a straight line through the origin?

Electron diffraction by nuclei

Nuclear radius

High-energy electrons diffract around nuclei. The angle of the first minimum tells us the nuclear radius. Change the nucleon number and the electron energy.

relative intensity against scattering angle

Key equations

R = R₀A1/3R₀ ≈ 1.05 fmsin θ = 0.61λ/Rλ ≈ hc/E

Breaking it down

  1. Electrons are leptons: they don't feel the strong force, so they probe the nucleus without being pulled in. Alphas are repelled long before reaching it.
  2. To diffract from something a few fm across, λ must be similar, so electrons need energies of hundreds of MeV. At these speeds E ≈ pc, so λ = h/p ≈ hc/E.
  3. The pattern has a central maximum and a first minimum at sin θ = 0.61λ/R. Intensity falls rapidly with angle but never quite reaches zero.
  4. Results give R = R₀A1/3. Plot R against A1/3: a straight line through the origin with gradient R₀. Or plot ln R against ln A: gradient ⅓.
  5. Volume ∝ R³ ∝ A, so all nuclei have about the same density, around 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³.

Common traps

  • Using λ = h/mv with relativistic electrons. Use E = hc/λ.
  • A1/3 not A/3. On a calculator use A^(1/3).
  • Nuclear density is much greater than atomic density because the atom is mostly empty space.

Try this: keep the energy fixed and increase A. Which way does the first minimum move, and why?

Mass and energy

Binding energy, fission and fusion

The binding energy per nucleon curve. Pick a nuclide to see its mass defect, or pick a reaction to see why energy is released as the products move up the curve.

binding energy per nucleon / MeV against nucleon number

Key equations

E = mc²1 u = 931.5 MeVΔm = Zmp + (A − Z)mn − mnucleusenergy released = BEproducts − BEreactants

Breaking it down

  1. A nucleus has less mass than its separate nucleons. This mass defect is the binding energy: the energy needed to pull the nucleus completely apart.
  2. Binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron-56 (about 8.8 MeV). Nuclei near the peak are the most stable.
  3. Fusion of light nuclei and fission of heavy nuclei both move towards the peak, so both release energy.
  4. Fusion needs very high temperatures so nuclei have enough kinetic energy to overcome electrostatic repulsion.
  5. Thermal reactor: fission of U-235 is induced by slow (thermal) neutrons. The moderator (water, graphite) slows neutrons by elastic collisions; control rods (boron) absorb neutrons; the coolant carries heat away. Critical mass: enough fuel for the chain reaction to sustain itself.

Common traps

  • Saying binding energy is energy stored in the nucleus. It is energy you'd need to supply to separate it.
  • Mixing atomic and nuclear masses: subtract electron masses if you're given atomic masses.
  • Energy released comes from the increase in total binding energy, not from "breaking bonds".
